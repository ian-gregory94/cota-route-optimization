Columbus transit optimization · Experiments 1–3

COTA's frequency allocation is close to optimal, and the first answer that said otherwise was wrong

Experiment 1 claimed a headway reallocation could cut unserved demand 32% at no cost. Rebuilding passenger assignment on RAPTOR and real origin-destination flows destroyed that result. The achievable gain is about 1% on both objectives, or 5.8% less unserved demand at 0.6% higher cost. The trunk-route cut that looked so implausible was an artifact, and the model gap that caused it turned out to be the one that mattered.

Superseded

Everything below Experiment 1's headline has been re-run. Two correctness bugs were also found and fixed along the way, both of which had been flattering every path-based number. Experiment 1's figures are kept here only as the thing that got corrected.

Which gap was doing the damage

Gap 1, passenger re-routing, was the whole story for route 10. Under route-level assignment a rider stranded by a frequency cut absorbed the full penalty, so the optimizer read the trunk routes as expensive to keep. Give travellers real origin-destination paths and let them switch, and the optimizer stops touching route 10 in every one of its six periods.

Gap 3, route classification, killed the express absurdity. Fourteen of COTA's 39 routes have no midday and no evening service at all — the 41–46, 51/52, 61 and 71–75 suburban commuter runs. Experiment 1 read their 180-minute "headways" as neglect and poured twelve times the service into them. Classified from their own service pattern and held at their designed timetable, that band now moves zero vehicle-hours.

Gap 2, crowding, turned out not to matter — and that is the finding. Priced properly at each route-period's busiest segment using real link loads, configurations B and C are nearly identical. COTA's buses are not full: see below.

Does crowding bind? No.

Measured at the peak load point from assigned link volumes, not route averages.

Experiment 1 applied crowding to a route's average passengers per trip, which never triggers. With path assignment the real answer is computable: each assigned trip rides a known span of a known pattern, so segment volumes accumulate and the maximum is the peak load point.

Across 35 route-periods carrying modelled load in the AM peak, the median bus at its busiest segment runs at 9% of capacity and the 90th percentile at 31%. Exactly one exceeds 60% — a single-trip suburban express that is locked at its designed timetable anyway.

NTD corroborates independently: COTA reports 13.0 unlinked trips per revenue hour across the whole bus mode. A bus that picks up 13 people an hour, each riding about a third of the route, is carrying roughly five passengers at a time. There is no capacity argument against reallocating trunk frequency at COTA's actual ridership — the argument has to come from re-routing, and it does.

The direction holds. The magnitude does not.

Service still moves from the frequent end of the ladder toward the sparse middle — but a fifth as much of it, and it now stops entirely at the commuter-express band.

Route 10 E Broad/W Broad, the test case

Resolved

Experiment 1 cut it from a 14.4-minute AM-peak headway to 60 minutes. Under every corrected configuration the optimizer leaves AM peak, midday and PM peak exactly where they are — 14.4, 15.0 and 15.0 minutes. Configuration A alone (re-routing, before any route classification or crowding) is enough: all six of its periods come back untouched.

Why the mechanism still points the same way

Expected wait is concave in headway, so a vehicle-hour still buys more at the bottom of the ladder than it costs at the top. What re-routing changes is the cost side: a rider losing frequency on a trunk route in a dense network has somewhere else to go, so cutting it is cheaper than the route-level model thought — but the riders who transfer onto those trunk routes from elsewhere now pay for the cut too, which the route-level model never counted. The second effect dominates.

Two bugs the verification caught

Both were silently flattering every path-based number.

The path-set approach caches candidate journeys and re-prices them as headways change. A cached set is a subset of what the router can find, so its cost must always be greater than or equal to a fresh RAPTOR run. The first verification pass showed the cache coming out cheaper — impossible, and therefore a bug.

Bug 1 · truncated journeys

Journey reconstruction walked parent pointers backward from the destination and returned whatever it had if the trace ran out. The legs it dropped are the ones nearest the origin, so those paths priced far below their true cost. It now returns nothing unless the back-walk actually reaches a source stop.

Bug 2 · route headway vs pattern headway

A ride leg was priced at its route's headway. But a route running several pattern variants is proportionally less frequent on each one, and a passenger riding a specific variant only benefits from that variant's trips. The router had this right; the evaluator did not — a constant 7.5-minute understatement on every one-transfer journey. Legs now carry a structural pattern-to-route frequency multiplier.

A third, smaller error surfaced with them: because labels carry between RAPTOR rounds, a traveller's first boarding could happen in round two and get charged a transfer penalty. Labels now track boarding count rather than round number.

Enumeration now rejects any candidate path that does not re-price to the cost the router reported for it. That check moved rejections from 64,382 to 235 and cut the cached set's cost error from −11.07% to −0.17%.

Verification

What was checked, and what the checks say.

Does the cached path set miss better journeys?

Full RAPTOR was re-run under both the baseline and the optimized plan and compared against the cache, OD pair by OD pair, across 19,557 AM-peak pairs. Mean disagreement: 0.17% at baseline, 0.09% optimized. Under the optimized plan 832 pairs (4.3%) have a better journey the cache does not hold. That is the assignment error the method introduces, and it is an order of magnitude smaller than the effects being measured.

Does the resource envelope actually hold?

Verified

The model's baseline vehicle-hours reproduce the GTFS schedule exactly — relative error 0.0 — because baseline headways are defined as k·T/n_trips. Every reported plan sits under 2,517.2 revenue vehicle-hours and under every per-period peak-vehicle cap. Every headway lies on its allowed ladder; no route-period loses service.

Experiment 1's plans were additionally re-evaluated by a second scalar implementation sharing no code with the vectorized solver. Maximum relative disagreement: 6.5 × 10⁻¹⁶.

Is the fleet constraint the right one?

NTD reports COTA operating 198 vehicles in maximum service. The schedule sweep finds 171 simultaneously-running trips and the model's continuous approximation 176.5. The real requirement is 12% above what the model constrains to, so the peak-fleet cap is conservative — the optimizer had less headroom than COTA actually has. That is the safe direction for a claim about staying inside current resources.

What is still missing

Two of the original five next steps are now done. These are what remain.

  1. Stop- and trip-level APC boardings. Still the binding constraint. Demand here is LODES commute flow — home to work, no non-work travel — scaled to an NTD boardings total. Only 24.7% of Central Ohio commute flow has a stop within walking range at both ends, and the model covers the top 65% of that. Observed boardings would replace all of it. Almost certainly a public records request.
  2. Sustained GTFS-Realtime collection. Feeds are public, the one-shot collector and SQLite schema are built and tested, the segment-runtime query is written. Nothing has been collected. It turns the reliability cost term from a zero-weighted placeholder into a measured quantity.
  3. Hyperpath assignment. A traveller is currently assigned to the single best pattern, not to the combined frequency of every pattern that would serve them. On this network 52 of 78 route-directions run one pattern and the dominant pattern carries 86% of trips, so the error is small — but it is an understatement of effective frequency throughout.
  4. Vehicle blocks from GTFS block_id. The field is present and unexploited. It yields a true peak-vehicle count including interlining, replacing the continuous cycle-over-headway approximation and closing the 171-vs-198 gap against NTD.
  5. Transfer timing. Now actually reachable. With OD paths in hand the next decision variable is not frequency but offset — holding headways fixed and shifting departure times so connections meet. The harness supports it; the experiment has not been run.
Standing caveat

None of this is a COTA recommendation. It is a model of COTA built from public data, calibrated where public data allows, and honest about the rest. The single most useful thing it has produced so far is the retraction of its own first answer.
