Columbus transit optimization · Experiment 1

COTA runs a steeper frequency ladder than passenger cost justifies

Holding route geometry, stop patterns and the scheduled resource envelope fixed, a reallocation of headways cuts the share of modelled demand that loses usable service access by 32% — at slightly lower total passenger generalized cost, using 97% of today's vehicle-hours. The direction survives every robustness test thrown at it. The specific plan is not implementable, and the reason why is the most useful thing in this report.

What the plan actually does

It flattens the frequency ladder — and that is the whole finding.

Grouped by how much service a route-period has today, the reallocation is almost monotone: hours come off everything already running every 15 minutes, and go to everything running worse than every 30.

Why this happens

Expected wait is concave in headway. Taking a route from a 15-minute to a 24-minute headway costs each rider about 1.5 minutes of expected wait. Taking a different route from 60 minutes to 24 saves each of its riders about 9 minutes — and recovers demand that the retention curve had written off entirely at 60. One vehicle-hour buys roughly six times more wait reduction at the bottom of the ladder than it costs at the top.

Why you should not act on this plan

The direction is robust. The prescription is not.

The tell

The λ = 0.5 plan cuts 10 E BROAD/W BROAD from a 14.4-minute AM-peak headway to 60 minutes, and raises 75, 43, 42 and 46 from 180-minute headways to 15–24 minutes in the peaks. No planner would sign that.

Three model gaps produce it, and each is a concrete next milestone:

  1. Passengers cannot re-route. Cost is computed per route-period, not per origin–destination path, so a rider stranded by a cut on route 10 is charged the full penalty instead of walking two blocks to route 2. Route-level assignment systematically overstates the value of spreading service. A RAPTOR or CSA implementation over the existing network abstraction fixes this.
  2. Crowding never binds. At the assumed 40,000 weekday linked trips, the busiest bus in the model carries far less than its 60-passenger planning capacity, so nothing pushes back on cutting a trunk route. Turning the crowding penalty off entirely changes the answer by 0.00 percentage points — proof the term is inert at this demand scale, and that the real trunk-route capacity constraint is simply absent.
  3. A 180-minute headway is not always bad service. Routes 42, 43, 46 and 75 run two peak trips by design — commuter expresses, not neglected corridors. The retention curve reads them as a coverage failure worth 12× more service. The model has no concept of "peak-only by design", and no route-type taxonomy to give it one.

There is also the demand proxy itself. It measures spatial access — LODES workers and jobs within 600 m of a stop, split among the routes serving it — which is flatter than real ridership. That was the obvious line of attack, so it was tested directly.

What survived the skeptic pass

Four independent attacks on the result. None reversed it.

2 · Does it depend on the cost weights?

Thirteen assumption variants — wait weight ±50%, transfer penalty removed, retention floor 0.10 to 0.50, crowding off, layover 25%, demand scale 20k–80k trips, jobs weighted double, catchment 400 m. Unserved-demand reduction ranged from −37.1% to −42.2% across all of them. Total generalized cost moved between −4.6% and +3.7%, so the cost sign is not robust — only the coverage gain is.

3 · Is it solver noise?

Four seeds: generalized cost changed by 0.430%, 0.439%, 0.458%, 0.461% (standard deviation 0.015 percentage points). The heuristic is not wandering.

4 · Is the arithmetic right?

Verified

Every reported plan was re-evaluated by a second, independent scalar implementation that shares no code with the vectorized solver. Maximum relative disagreement across cost, unserved demand, vehicle-hours and peak vehicles: 6.5 × 10⁻¹⁶ — floating-point noise.

The model's baseline vehicle-hours reproduce the GTFS scheduled vehicle-hours exactly (relative error 0.0), because baseline headways are defined as k·T/n_trips. There is no calibration gap. Every peak-vehicle cap is respected in all six periods; every headway lies on its allowed ladder; no route-period lost service.

Two data errors the skeptic pass caught before they reached the result

Unscaled demand. The first run showed an −11% cost improvement. Turning off the crowding penalty collapsed baseline cost by two-thirds, which meant crowding was dominating the objective. The cause: the LODES proxy's raw units are "workers + jobs near a stop" (~940,000), which modelled every bus as carrying 400 passengers. The proxy is now rescaled to an explicit weekday linked-trip assumption, and that assumption is swept in the sensitivity table. The −11% was an artifact and is gone.

Kilometres reported as miles. GTFS does not fix the unit of shape_dist_traveled. COTA's feed uses kilometres. Taken at face value it inflates scheduled vehicle-miles by 61%. The units are now inferred by comparison against the projected shape geometry — the two independent methods agree to 0.16% once converted (38,471 vs 38,531 miles).

What would make this credible

Ranked by how much each unlocks, not by effort.

  1. Stop- and trip-level APC boardings — replaces the proxy with observed demand. Almost certainly requires a public records request; it is the precondition for calling any of this a real COTA result.
  2. The NTD agency profile for COTA — reconciles 2,517 scheduled vehicle-hours and 38,531 vehicle-miles against reported actuals. The cheapest validation available, and it needs no records request.
  3. Sustained GTFS-Realtime collection — the feeds are public and the collector is built. Empirical segment runtime distributions turn the reliability cost term from a zero-weighted placeholder into a measured quantity, and show where schedule padding is misallocated.
  4. RAPTOR or CSA over the existing network abstraction — removes model gap #1 above, and is the only way transfer timing becomes optimizable at all.
  5. Vehicle blocks from GTFS block_id — the field is present and unexploited. It yields a true peak-vehicle count including interlining, instead of the continuous cycle-over-headway approximation used here.
